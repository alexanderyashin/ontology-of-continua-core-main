## The Local Problem In distribution boundary Policy
The distribution boundary Policy is the first discipline of transmission in Ontology of Continua. It says that a claim formed in one setting must not be carried into another setting merely because the same language sounds useful there. Before the book compares different kinds of order, breakdown, or change, it must make clear how comparison is allowed to happen.
Four terms are needed at the start. A continuum is a structured range of possible states or descriptions. A boundary is a distinction that makes some transitions, measurements, or identities meaningful. An operator is an action or transformation that changes a configuration within stated limits. A projection is a stated mapping from one continuum into another. It names what is preserved, what is lost, and what must be reinterpreted.
With those terms in place, the policy can be stated plainly: a claim may travel only by projection. It cannot travel by atmosphere, resemblance, or enthusiasm. The point is not to prevent comparison. The point is to prevent a comparison from pretending to be stronger than it is.
## The Necessary Distinction In distribution boundary Policy
Consider a chemical reaction network, a city district, and a research institution. Each can show coordination. Each can reach thresholds. Each can break down. But the molecules in a reaction network, the residents of a district, and the departments of an institution are not interchangeable units. Their boundaries are drawn differently, their operators act differently, and their continuums have different observables.
An unsendable claim would say: because an autocatalytic chemical network can maintain itself through reaction closure, a research institution maintains itself by the same mechanism. That sentence moves too quickly. It has not said what counts as a reaction in the institution, what counts as catalysis, what closure would mean, or how the receiving continuum is measured. The claim may be suggestive, but it is not yet licensed.
A properly projected claim is more modest and more useful. It might say: in the chemical case, closure means that reactions generate and sustain the components needed for further reactions. In the institutional case, a possible analogue would require roles, procedures, and decisions that reproduce the conditions for future roles, procedures, and decisions. The projection preserves the pattern of mutual enablement, but it does not preserve the material mechanism. It also loses the exact chemistry. What remains is a structured comparison, not an imported proof.

The sciences of self-organization make this restraint necessary rather than optional. Hermann Haken’s synergetics showed that systems made of many interacting parts can sometimes be described through collective variables. Local interactions may produce macroscopic order, and that order can guide the behavior of the parts. This gives a serious reason to look for recurring forms across different settings.
Nicolis and Prigogine deepened the lesson in far-from-equilibrium systems. They studied how flows of matter and energy can support organized structures that would not appear in closed equilibrium conditions. Their work matters here because it shows that order is not always imposed from outside. Under certain constraints, it can arise through the dynamics of the system itself.
Kauffman’s work on autocatalytic organization, and later formal work on RAF networks, added another important thread. These studies examined how networks of reactions can become mutually sustaining: components help generate other components, and the network gains a kind of closure. This is powerful language, but it is also dangerous if lifted carelessly. A chemical closure result does not automatically become a theory of institutions, minds, cities, or histories.
Taken together, these traditions justify pattern-seeking. They do not justify domain erasure. They show why recurring structures deserve attention, while also showing why every transfer must preserve its conditions. Similarity can begin an inquiry. It cannot finish one.
## An Objection Worth Keeping In distribution boundary Policy
The distribution boundary Policy protects meaning by requiring a visible projection before a claim is reused. A projection is not a decorative bridge between examples. It is the disciplined work of saying how a source continuum relates to a receiving continuum. It states the source, the destination, and the terms of passage.
This matters because strong words can become weightless when they travel without their setting. Boundary, operator, contradiction, emergence, projection, and collapse are useful only when the reader can see where they are defined. A boundary in a physical model may be a measurable interface. A boundary in an institution may be a jurisdiction, role, procedure, or recognized distinction. These may be comparable, but they are not identical by default.
The policy therefore turns generality into a testable ambition. A general claim is not one that floats above all contexts. It is one whose stable features can be named across contexts without pretending that all contexts share the same units, measurements, or mechanisms. The stronger the claim, the more explicit its projection must be.

A natural worry is that this makes the theory timid. If every movement from one continuum to another requires projection, perhaps the book loses the ability to speak broadly. But breadth without conditions is not strength. It is vagueness wearing the costume of universality.
The distribution boundary Policy does not forbid large claims. It asks large claims to show their path. If a later argument speaks about collapse across physical, biological, cognitive, social, and formal settings, it must say what collapse means in each receiving continuum. It must name the relevant boundaries, the operators that can change the configuration, and the signs by which failure or transition is recognized. Only then can the comparison become more than a borrowed word.
The policy also has a clear limit. It does not prove that a projection is correct. It only makes the projection available for inspection. The work of evidence remains. Definitions must be tested against their intended domains. Models must not be treated as observations. Formal resemblance must not be mistaken for empirical sameness. A visible projection is the beginning of disciplined movement, not the end of inquiry.
