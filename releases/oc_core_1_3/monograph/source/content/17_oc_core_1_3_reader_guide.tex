\section{Reader Contract and Navigation Guide}
\label{sec:oc13-reader-contract}

Core 1.3 is one flagship scientific object, but it is meant to be entered by
more than one serious reader. This guide therefore acts as a navigation
contract rather than as a second introduction. Its job is to show which route
serves which reader, where the proof burden lives, where the audit burden
lives, and where practical or external use should begin.

\subsection{The five lawful reading routes}

\textbf{Reviewer route.}
Read the front matter, this guide, Chapters~18--20, and then
Appendices~D--F. That route exposes the publication boundary, theorem fate,
load-bearing proof spine, reviewer objections, and source witnesses as quickly
as possible.

\textbf{Formal route.}
Read Chapters~1--6 only as orientation, then study Chapters~8--15 and
18--25 in order, followed by Appendices~A--C and Appendix~M, and then the
technical derivation atlas in Appendix~O when a local proof-support leaf is
needed. This is the shortest route that keeps exact doctrine, theorem scope,
and proof machinery visible at once.

\textbf{Domain route.}
Read Chapters~14 and 21--26, then move into Appendices~G--L and Appendix~R.
That path exposes bounded domain embedding, worked interpretation, empirical
promotion rules, replay surfaces, benchmark cases, execution board, practical
use, and serious comparators without forcing the reader to traverse every
technical derivation leaf first.

\textbf{Broad scientific route.}
Read Chapters~1--6, 8--15, and 18--26 straight through as a continuous
graduate-level monograph. Use Appendix~A for notation when needed and defer
Appendices~N--R until a concrete figure, benchmark, or atlas question arises.
This is the recommended route for a strong non-specialist reader.

\textbf{Practical and external route.}
Read the front matter, this guide, Chapter~26, and Appendix~R first. Return
to Chapters~22--25 only when a practical row needs its theorem, replay, or
falsifier support inspected in detail. This is the shortest lawful route for
an external collaborator asking what OC now lets one predict, do, compare, or
build within declared bounds.

\subsection{What each major block is for}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Orientation and scientific promise (Chapters~1--6).} Problem,
      background, model, present result contour, discussion, and conclusion.
\item \textbf{Formal core and structural doctrine (Chapters~8--15).} Boundary,
      thresholds, K-level doctrine, operators, collapse and rebirth,
      branching, disciplinary embedding, and falsifiability logic.
\item \textbf{Core 1.3 closure, synthesis, and practical use (Chapters~18--26).}
      Publication boundary, foundational consistency, theorem navigation,
      worked examples, operational and empirical closure discipline, proof
      machinery, TOE synthesis, and practical consequences.
\item \textbf{Reader-facing audit appendices (Appendices~A--M).} Notation,
      axiomatic support, K-level tables, parameter and numerical tables,
      source-audit and reviewer matrices, empirical validation surfaces,
      benchmark manifest and caseset, replay reports, execution board, and
      proof-machinery appendix.
\item \textbf{Atlas and support appendices (Appendices~N--R).} Figure atlas,
      technical derivation atlas, reference and benchmark atlas, TOE support
      dossiers, and the practical utility and model comparison atlas.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What this guide is not}

This guide does not restate the theory, replace the theorem roadmap, or turn
the flagship monograph into a dashboard. It simply tells the reader where each
task belongs. Chapter~20 owns proof navigation. Chapter~25 owns synthesis.
Chapter~26 owns practical consequences and serious comparison. The appendices
keep the inspection burden inside the same volume without displacing the main
reading spine.
